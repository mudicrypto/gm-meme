# Build stops without ever holding keys

Sift can offer limit, take-profit, stop-loss and trailing-stop orders without taking custody of user keys, but not in the way GMGN does it. GMGN's orders work while you sleep because GMGN generates and controls the trading wallet, and its own docs call that wallet "custodial" ([GMGN docs](https://docs.gmgn.ai/index/tg-wallet-connect-tg-multi-wallet-system)). Every peer that offers orders which fire while the user is offline has given some party other than the user's phone the power to sign at the moment the price is hit. For Sift the honest path has two stages. First, build **"armed alerts"**: Sift's server watches the price, sends a push notification and has the trade ready, and the user signs it with one tap. This keeps every key on the phone, works on devnet and on pump.fun bonding curves today, and reuses the price-alert code Sift already has. Second, on mainnet, add truly automatic orders through one of two routes. One is **Jupiter's Trigger Order API V2**, which supports stop-loss, take-profit pairs and trailing stops, but its developer docs say tokens wait in a "custodial" vault managed by Privy. The other is a **Swig smart account**, where the phone's key stays the master key and Sift's server gets a narrow, expiring permission enforced by an on-chain program. No route can promise that a stop-loss on a thin meme coin will fill near the trigger: rugs, frozen tokens and sandwich bots defeat stops, whatever the design. **Nothing described here has been built**; this is research only. The regulatory points summarise public sources and are **not legal advice**. A UAE lawyer needs to review them, especially since a new federal law may reach software that merely "facilitates" financial activity.

## Every offline stop on the market works because someone else can sign

A stop-loss has to sell at 3 a.m. while the user's phone is in a drawer. On Solana, only a valid signature can move tokens, so whoever sells at 3 a.m. must be able to sign. The way each competitor handles that signature decides whether it is custodial.

GMGN offers limit buys ("Buy Dip"), limit sells by amount or percentage of holdings, several take-profit (TP) and stop-loss (SL) levels attached to a buy, trailing take-profit, trailing stop-loss, "migrated snipe" at pump.fun graduation, dev-sell triggers and copy trading ([GMGN docs: Auto Sell & TP/SL](https://docs.gmgn.ai/index/auto-sell-auto-sell-take-profit-stop-loss); [Trailing Stop Loss](https://docs.gmgn.ai/index/trailing-stop-loss); [Migrated Snipe](https://docs.gmgn.ai/index/auto-trading-migrated-snipe)). A trailing stop means the sell price follows the token up and never moves down: stop = highest price × (1 − drawdown %). The bot docs list order lifetimes of 1 hour, 24 hours or 3 days. GMGN charges a **flat 1% per trade**, plus pump.fun's own 1% on pump.fun tokens. It recommends **30–35% slippage** for automated orders (50% or more for hot new tokens) and at least **0.006 SOL** in priority fees for limit orders ([GMGN docs: Fees & Settings](https://docs.gmgn.ai/index/gmgn-fees-settings)). Slippage is the worst price drop from the quote that the user accepts. Under the hood, a GMGN "limit order" is a server-held instruction to fire a normal market swap when a price is seen. GMGN's own blog says that once triggered, these orders "fill at your slippage… neither can lock the price 100%" ([GMGN blog](https://gmgn.ai/blog/how-to-set-take-profit-stop-loss-on-gmgn/), seen only as a search snippet). Its failure page warns that price "pins" (brief spikes) may not trigger an order, and that a triggered order can fail on slippage and still burn the fee ([GMGN docs: Limit Order failure](https://docs.gmgn.ai/index/transaction-limit-order-failure)).

All of this requires a GMGN-made wallet. "Trade with plugin wallet doesn't support these features." Logging in with Phantom makes GMGN "create a multi-chain custodial wallet account" for you, and private keys cannot be exported on any chain ([GMGN docs: Multi-Wallet](https://docs.gmgn.ai/index/tg-wallet-connect-tg-multi-wallet-system); [Import & Export](https://docs.gmgn.ai/index/tg-wallet-import-export-private-key-deposit-withdraw)). The weakness of that model showed in **October 2025**. Phishers stole GMGN login session tokens and made GMGN's servers spend about **107 users'** SOL buying honeypot tokens, which can be bought but not sold. Losses exceeded **$700K**, even though no private key leaked ([Odaily](https://www.odaily.news/en/newsflash/454353); [Phemex News](https://phemex.com/news/article/gmgn-users-targeted-in-700000-phishing-scheme-exploiting-pi-xiu-pools-30761)). When the server signs, the login screen becomes the vault door.

Peers vary the same pattern. Axiom builds on Turnkey, a key-management service that holds keys in secure hardware enclaves (sealed, tamper-resistant computing environments). Axiom shows users an exportable recovery phrase, and its CTO credits Turnkey's policy engine with letting it "automate onchain actions without compromising security" ([Turnkey: Axiom case study](https://turnkey.com/customers/axiom-global-defi-trading-platform); [Axiom docs](https://docs.axiom.trade/getting-started/signup)). Axiom's fees run from **0.95% down to 0.75%** ([Axiom fees](https://docs.axiom.trade/getting-started/fees/axiom-fees)). Bloom's limit orders "run on Bloom's infrastructure", meaning the bot watches and executes ([MadeOnSol](https://madeonsol.com/blog/how-to-use-bloom-trading-bot), an affiliate source). Pump.fun itself added TP/SL orders in its app in **September 2026**, wallet model undisclosed ([Phemex News](https://phemex.com/news/article/pumpfun-launches-solana-limit-orders-with-takeprofit-and-stoploss-tools-95256)). The research found no peer that signs at trigger time on the user's own device. So "GMGN-style" orders always mean the provider, its vendor or its vault can move the funds.

## Jupiter's trigger API does everything, but parks tokens in a vault Jupiter calls custodial

Jupiter's current product is the **Trigger Order API V2** (shown in the jup.ag interface as "Limit Order V2"). It is the closest thing to a ready-made backend for Sift. It supports single orders (buy or sell above or below a USD price, which covers limit orders and stop-loss), **OCO** ("one cancels the other": a TP and an SL sharing one deposit) and **OTOCO** (an entry order that then arms an OCO pair) ([Create Order](https://developers.jup.ag/docs/trigger/create-order.md)). **Trailing stop-loss** shipped in July 2026, set as 0.5%–90% from the high, but "Trailing Take Profit is not supported" ([Changelog July 2026](https://developers.jup.ag/changelog/2026-07); [user docs](https://docs.jup.ag/user-docs/trade/spot/limit-orders)). Triggers use USD price only, and market-cap triggers are just a conversion in the frontend ([Trigger overview](https://developers.jup.ag/docs/trigger/index.md)). The old claim that Jupiter orders "cannot be used as stop losses" describes V1 and is out of date.

The catch is custody. The flow works like this. The user signs a login challenge. Sift asks Jupiter for the user's vault. The phone signs a deposit transaction that moves the tokens from the wallet into that vault. From then on, Jupiter's "keeper" (an automated executor) sells from the vault when the price hits, with no further user signature ([Vault & Deposit](https://developers.jup.ag/docs/trigger/deposit.md); [Manage Orders](https://developers.jup.ag/docs/trigger/manage-orders.md)). Jupiter's developer docs describe that vault as a **"Vault account (custodial) by Privy"**, "a Privy-managed custodial account". Its user docs call the same vault "program-owned, so there is no private key for it" ([Trigger overview](https://developers.jup.ag/docs/trigger/index.md); [user docs](https://docs.jup.ag/user-docs/trade/spot/limit-orders)). Until Jupiter clarifies in writing, Sift should treat V2 as **funds held by a third party while an order is open**. Sift itself would still never hold a key, but it could not honestly call these orders non-custodial. Jupiter's engineering blog confirms that order details (output token, trigger price) live in Jupiter's database, and that retries on failure are "best-effort rather than absolute" ([Jupiter blog, 2026-06-18](https://developers.jup.ag/blog/lov2-correctness-under-failure)). The older V1 used on-chain escrow accounts that only the user could withdraw from, but it never supported stop-loss, and jup.ag no longer creates V1 orders ([user docs](https://docs.jup.ag/user-docs/trade/spot/limit-orders)).

Pump.fun tokens are covered in principle, both before and after graduation. Jupiter's router lists both "Pump.fun" (the bonding curve, the automatic price formula a new token trades on until it sells out) and "Pump.fun AMM" (PumpSwap, the exchange a token moves to when it graduates). When a token graduates, the new market is added to routing, and a bonding curve that never graduates is removed after a grace period ([Jupiter: Market Listing](https://developers.jup.ag/docs/swap/routing/amm/market-listing.md)). Because the keeper chooses the route at fill time, an order placed before graduation should still fill afterwards. No Jupiter page says explicitly that trigger orders work on bonding-curve tokens, so this is an inference that needs a small mainnet test. A real gap for meme coins: a token's USD price "needs recent swap activity" to exist in Jupiter's price stream at all, so a stop on a dead or brand-new coin may never fire ([Jupiter blog, 2026-06-03](https://developers.jup.ag/blog/lov2-the-any-any-problem)).

The commercial terms are tight. The minimum order is **$10**. Fees are **0.03–0.1% plus a 0–0.5% routing fee**, taken at execution. Every order must have an expiry, with 30 days the recommended maximum. Stop-loss slippage **defaults to 20%**. Most importantly for Sift's business, integrator fees are **"Not currently [supported], and there is no timeline"**, so Sift's platform fee would need a separate transfer that the user signs at deposit time ([Trigger overview FAQ](https://developers.jup.ag/docs/trigger/index.md); [Best Practices](https://developers.jup.ag/docs/trigger/best-practices.md); [user docs](https://docs.jup.ag/user-docs/trade/spot/limit-orders)). The API is labelled **beta**, and no devnet support appears anywhere in its docs, so it cannot be tested before Sift's Step 8 security review and the move to mainnet ([Best Practices](https://developers.jup.ag/docs/trigger/best-practices.md); [docs index](https://developers.jup.ag/docs/llms.txt)). There is one more engineering wrinkle. Sift's phone checks every transaction against a strict allow-list before signing. A V2 deposit goes to a per-user vault address that Jupiter's API supplies, so the phone would have to trust an address it cannot check against a fixed list.

## Keeping keys on the phone means choosing which weakness to live with

Four self-built routes keep the user's key on the phone. Each trades away something different.

**Armed alerts (notify and sign).** Sift's API already checks price alerts and pushes a notification when one is crossed (`apps/api/src/social/alerts.ts`). Extending this so the alert carries a ready-built sell, which the user reviews and signs in one tap, is fully non-custodial. It works for embedded-wallet, Phantom and Mobile Wallet Adapter users alike, runs on devnet, and works on the pump.fun bonding curve today. Its weakness is plain: it is not a stop-loss if the user is asleep. It should be sold as "alerts with a one-tap trade", never as protection.

**Pre-signed sells with durable nonces.** A Solana transaction normally expires after about **80–90 seconds**. A "durable nonce" is a small on-chain account (about **0.0015 SOL** rent) that lets a signed transaction stay valid until it is used or cancelled ([Solana docs](https://solana.com/developers/guides/advanced/introduction-to-durable-nonces)). The phone could sign "sell X tokens for at least Y SOL" in advance, and Sift's keeper would broadcast it when the trigger hits. The keeper cannot change the deal, but four problems follow. First, the minimum price is fixed at signing, so if a coin gaps straight through it (common in rugs), the stop simply fails. Second, whoever holds the signed transaction can send it early, and a stop-loss's minimum is below today's price, so early submission sells at a loss. Third, the transaction is pinned to exact pump.fun accounts and instruction versions, and pump.fun has been changing those (new `buy_v2/sell_v2` and `v3` instructions, new mandatory fee accounts) ([pump-public-docs](https://github.com/pump-fun/pump-public-docs)). Graduation also moves the market, which strands any pre-signed bonding-curve sell. Fourth, durable nonces were the tool behind the **~$270M Drift exploit in April 2026**, in which signers were tricked into pre-signing admin transactions that were executed later ([CoinDesk](https://www.coindesk.com/tech/2026/04/02/how-a-solana-feature-designed-for-convenience-let-an-attacker-drain-usd270-million-from-drift)). Teaching users to sign long-lived transactions is a phishing risk.

**A Sift escrow program.** Sift could write its own on-chain program. The user deposits tokens into an account the program controls, and anyone (Sift's keeper, or a public "crank" service such as Helium's Tuk Tuk, which needs a **1 SOL** refundable deposit per task queue) can call "execute". The program checks the price and the minimum output, then sells through pump.fun, PumpSwap or Jupiter ([Tuk Tuk README](https://github.com/helium/tuktuk)). This is what Jupiter V1 was, extended with stop-loss. Done well, with an immutable program or an upgrade key under a timelocked multisig, and withdrawals only to the user, it is the strongest non-custodial design. It is also the most dangerous to build. Bugs in account and signature checks caused Solana's biggest losses, including Wormhole (~$326M) and Cashio (~$52M), and Mango Markets lost ~$116M to price manipulation ([Helius: Solana Hacks](https://www.helius.dev/blog/solana-hacks)). The program would need a professional audit, a trustworthy price source (pool prices can be pushed around inside a single transaction) and constant upkeep as pump.fun changes. Its open orders would also be publicly visible. Jupiter abandoned that model partly because "MEV bots can front-run triggers" ([Jupiter blog](https://developers.jup.ag/blog/lov2-correctness-under-failure)).

**Token approvals and other venues.** Solana token accounts let an owner "Approve" a delegate to move up to N tokens ([SPL Token docs](https://www.solana-program.com/docs/token)). That delegate can send those tokens anywhere, so it amounts to custody of that amount and is ruled out. Meteora's DLMM pools offer true on-chain limit orders with no keeper, but only on pools built for them and only on the "resting" side. That allows take-profit and buy-the-dip orders, never stop-loss, and pump.fun coins graduate to PumpSwap rather than Meteora ([Meteora docs](https://docs.meteora.ag/core-products/dlmm/limit-order)). Drift's docs (now "Velocity Protocol") say spot orders are "rejected onchain" ([Drift docs](https://docs.drift.trade/protocol/trading/order-types/advanced-order-types)). Clockwork, once Solana's automation service, shut down in 2023 ([QuickNode](https://www.quicknode.com/guides/solana-development/legacy/automation-with-clockwork)).

## Delegated signing splits into "move the key" versus "keep the key and grant a pass"

For the embedded wallet there is a fourth family: give Sift's server a **limited, revocable permission** to sign certain trades. This is how Axiom-style apps automate. It comes in two forms.

**Vendor-enclave signers (Privy, Turnkey, and others).** Privy's docs name the use case directly: "execute limit orders or agentic trades even while a user is offline". The user adds a "signer", a key held by Sift's server, to their wallet with a policy attached. Privy checks every Solana instruction against that policy inside AWS Nitro enclaves, denies anything not explicitly allowed, and the user can remove all signers at any time ([Privy signers](https://docs.privy.io/wallets/using-wallets/signers/overview.md); [Privy policies](https://docs.privy.io/controls/policies/overview.md); [remove signers](https://docs.privy.io/wallets/using-wallets/signers/remove-signers.md)). Turnkey's "Delegated Access" works the same way and names "limit orders" too. Its policy language can inspect Solana programs, accounts and token transfers, which makes it the most expressive engine reviewed ([Turnkey delegated access](https://docs.turnkey.com/features/policies/delegated-access/overview); [policy language](https://docs.turnkey.com/features/policies/language)). Both support Expo/React Native. The structural cost for Sift is that **the key leaves the phone**. Sift's existing seed would be imported into Privy's split-key system or Turnkey's enclaves ([Privy import](https://docs.privy.io/wallets/wallets/import-a-wallet/private-key.md); [Turnkey import](https://docs.turnkey.com/features/wallets/import-wallets)). Sift would still never see a key, but its promise that "your key lives only on your phone" would end, and the product copy would have to say so. Pricing as of October 2026: Privy is free up to **500 monthly users and 50K signatures**, then **$299** or **$499 per month** by user tier ([Privy pricing](https://www.privy.io/pricing)). Turnkey charges **$0.10 per signature** pay-as-you-go, or **$99 a month plus $0.05**, with lower tiers capped at 5 policies ([Turnkey pricing](https://www.turnkey.com/pricing)). Privy now belongs to Stripe, which announced the acquisition in June 2025 ([Blockworks](https://blockworks.co/news/stripe-acquires-privy-wallet)). Dynamic and Coinbase CDP offer similar Solana delegation, but Dynamic requires an Enterprise plan and hands the developer an actual key share ([Dynamic](https://www.dynamic.xyz/docs/embedded-wallets/mpc/delegated-access/overview.md); [CDP](https://docs.cdp.coinbase.com/wallets/using-wallets/delegated-signing.md)).

**On-chain smart accounts (Swig, Squads).** A smart account is a wallet whose rules are enforced by a Solana program, not by a vendor's servers. With **Swig**, Sift's existing phone key becomes the account's master ("root") key, so no import is needed and no vendor holds anything. The server gets a role or a time-limited session restricted to named programs, with token spending limits. The phone removes that role with one on-chain transaction ([Swig sessions](https://build.onswig.com/protocol/sessions-and-subaccounts.md); [Swig permissions](https://build.onswig.com/developer-sdk/permissions.md)). Swig has a 2025 audit by Accretion and an Expo SDK whose default network is devnet ([Swig audits](https://build.onswig.com/protocol/security-and-audits.md); [Swig Expo](https://build.onswig.com/idp-sdk/expo-and-react-native.md)). The cost is a migration: the user's funds move from their current address to the smart account's address. Squads' Smart Account Program is live on mainnet and devnet but labelled v0.1, "in active development", and the research found no audit of that specific program ([Squads README](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/Squads-Protocol/smart-account-program/main/README.md)). Lazorkit says "Pre-audit. Do not use in production" ([Lazorkit README](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/lazor-kit/lazor-kit/main/README.md)).

Two caveats apply to every delegated design. First, **Phantom and Mobile Wallet Adapter users cannot delegate at all**. A plain Solana wallet has a single key and no built-in "session key" feature, so for them automatic orders require Jupiter V2, a Sift escrow program, or armed alerts. Second, Sift's strongest current safeguard, the phone checking every transaction with `verifyTradeTransaction` before signing, does not run when the server signs at 3 a.m. That check must move into the policy itself. The research found **no vendor or Swig policy confirmed to enforce "minimum output ≥ X"** on a swap. A program allow-list plus a spending cap limits how much a hacked Sift server could touch. It does not stop that server from selling the user's tokens at a terrible price into a sandwich bot. The policy therefore needs to pin the token being sold, the destination (the user's own account) and the amount, and expire quickly.

## Meme-coin stops fail in ways no architecture fixes

Even a perfect, non-custodial stop-loss is a weak promise on pump.fun coins. In a study of **34,988 meme tokens**, about **82.8%** of high-return tokens showed artificial price inflation, and Solana tokens were manipulated earliest, a median of 62 days after creation ([arXiv 2507.01963](https://arxiv.org/abs/2507.01963v2)). Fewer than 2% of pump.fun launches ever reach a DEX ([Solana Compass](https://solanacompass.com/news/pumpfun-launched-42000-tokens-in-one-day-fewer-than-2-will-ever-reach-a-dex)). On thin markets, one dump can trip a cluster of stops, a practice called "stop hunting", after which the manipulator buys back cheaply. In a rug (liquidity pulled), a freeze (the token's creator blocks transfers) or a honeypot (selling blocked or heavily taxed), a stop either fails or fills near zero. Jupiter itself warns that "Stop Loss is not guaranteed to execute… especially common with low-liquidity tokens such as new memecoins or tokens that have been rugged" ([Jupiter user docs](https://docs.jup.ag/user-docs/trade/spot/limit-orders)).

MEV ("maximal extractable value", here meaning bots that profit by reordering trades) makes triggered sells worse. In a sandwich attack, a bot buys just ahead of a trade and sells just after it, pocketing up to the slippage the victim allowed. Helius found one sandwich bot earning about **758 SOL a day**, with 16 of its top 20 targets being pump.fun tokens, and noted that meme traders are "especially susceptible" because they set wide slippage ([Helius MEV report](https://www.helius.dev/blog/solana-mev-report)). A stop-loss firing at GMGN's recommended 30–35% slippage, or Jupiter's 20% default, is close to an ideal target. Helius's opt-in MEV Protect avoids validators linked to sandwiching but promises only "a much lower chance" ([Helius MEV Protect](https://www.helius.dev/docs/sending-transactions/mev-protect)). Exchanges handle the trade-off by offering two order types. A **stop-market** order executes at an uncertain price. A **stop-limit** order holds a worst acceptable price but "may remain open" if the market jumps past it ([Bitvavo](https://support.bitvavo.com/hc/en/articles/4405191439633)). Sift's rule to show full cost before any trade points to stop-limit as the default. The user would see the worst-case fill (trigger price minus slippage minus fees) when arming the order, plus a clear warning that the order may never fill on a rug or freeze. Tokens with no reliable price data would show amber "may not trigger", in line with Sift's rule that missing data is never green.

Security history points the same way. Operator-held hot keys are the recurring source of trading-bot losses: Banana Gun (**$3M**, 2024), BONKbot and Solareum (**~$520K**), and GMGN's session-token incident ([The Block](https://www.theblock.co/post/318074/banana-gun-exploit-refund); [ForkLog](https://forklog.com/en/telegram-bots-bonkbot-and-solareum-users-suffer-520000-losses-due-to-hacks/)). Insiders are a risk too: in February 2026 ZachXBT alleged that Axiom staff used internal tools to look up users' wallets and front-run them ([The Block](https://www.theblock.co/post/391423/zachxbt-investigation-into-alleged-employee-insider-trading)). Any design in which Sift's server can sign or see pending orders creates both an attack surface and an insider temptation.

## Regulators ask one question: who can move the money

_This section summarises public, mostly secondary sources. It is not legal advice._ The regimes researched share one test: does the operator control, or co-control, the means of moving the user's assets? In Dubai, VARA's Custody Services Rulebook (version 2.0, effective 19 June 2025) is described by local law firms as covering "receiving, safeguarding, or controlling" client assets, with signing control or co-control treated as safekeeping. Custody must sit in a separate licensed entity ([VARA rulebook](https://rulebooks.vara.ae/node/257); [Al Safar & Partners](https://www.linkedin.com/pulse/vara-custody-services-rulebook-alsafarandpartners-lawfirm)). Executing users' conditional orders could also look like VARA's Broker-Dealer activity, which covers arranging and executing orders ([ABS Partners](https://abspartners.ae/vara-licensed-activities-dubai-regulatory-framework-guide/)). The research found **no VARA guidance that defines or exempts non-custodial wallet software**. Abu Dhabi's ADGM rulebook says non-custodial wallet providers "would generally not be required" to get a custody licence "because they have no control over client assets", but that text was seen only as a search snippet ([ADGM rulebook](https://en.adgm.thomsonreuters.com/rulebook/custodial-arrangements-clients-virtual-assets)). The biggest new factor is federal. Commentators report that Federal Decree-Law No. 6 of 2025 (the new Central Bank law) ended its transition period on **16 September 2026**, and read its Article 62 as reaching anyone who "facilitates" a licensed financial activity "by any means, medium or technology", with penalties up to **AED 1 billion** ([Al Maazmi Lawyers](https://www.almaazmilawyers.com/insights/banking/2026-05-18-federal-decree-on-central-bank-financial-institutions-and-in)). If that reading holds, "we only write software" may not be a defence in the UAE. The primary text was not retrieved.

Elsewhere the lines are similar. US FinCEN's 2019 guidance treats unhosted-wallet software as outside money transmission, and a provider that adds only a second, owner-requested approval key lacks "total independent control" ([Katten summary](https://katten.com/fincen-publishes-guidance-pertaining-to-certain-business-models-involving-convertible-virtual-currencies)). EU commentators read MiCA's custody definition as turning on who holds signing and recovery authority ([Dudkowiak](https://www.dudkowiak.com/fintech-in-poland/crypto-services-under-mica/)). Google Play exempts non-custodial wallets from its licensing rule ([Decrypt](https://decrypt.co/335134/google-non-custodial-wallets-exempt-new-crypto-app-rules-play-store)). Apple's guideline 3.1.5(iii) requires "appropriate licensing" in each country for apps that facilitate exchange transactions ([Apple guidelines](https://developer.apple.com/app-store/review/guidelines/)). On these tests, a GMGN-style wallet is clearly custodial. Delegated signing is a grey zone that depends on how narrow and revocable the permission is. Pre-signed transactions look closest to "the owner keeps control", but the keeper still decides when to execute. A Sift escrow program with upgrade or admin powers may count as co-control. Jupiter V2 moves custody to Jupiter and Privy, yet Sift could still be seen as introducing users to a service that Jupiter itself labels custodial.

## Recommendation: ship armed alerts now, pilot one automatic route on devnet

Build **Option A first**, on devnet, as the next trading feature. It honours every Sift rule, works for all three wallet types and on bonding-curve tokens, and builds on code that already exists. Label it "price alerts with one-tap trades", never "stop-loss protection". Make stop-limit (worst price shown) the default, show the full cost at arming and at signing, and mark tokens with thin or missing price data as amber "may not trigger".

For orders that fire automatically, the leading candidate for embedded-wallet users is **Option F, Swig sessions**. It is the only delegated design that keeps the phone's key as the master key with no vendor holding anything, and it can be tested on devnet. Any rollout should wait for a lawyer's view and for proof that the policy pins the token sold, the destination and the amount, and that sessions expire quickly. **Option B, Jupiter V2**, is the fastest way to full GMGN parity on mainnet after Step 8, and it covers Phantom users too. Offer it only as a clearly labelled opt-in ("your tokens wait in Jupiter's vault until the order fills or you cancel"), and only after Jupiter confirms the vault's custody model in writing. Accept that Sift earns no fee on those fills except through a separate signed transfer. Keep Option E as the fallback if Swig proves too immature. Its cost is a rewritten key-storage promise, so the copy must change before the code does. Reject options C and G, and defer D unless Sift ever wants to fund an audited on-chain program.

## Conclusion

The research turns "add GMGN-style orders" from a feature request into a custody decision. The order types themselves are simple: a price watcher plus a market swap. The hard part is the signature at 3 a.m., and every competitor solves it by moving signing power off the user's device. Sift's advantage is that it can say truthfully that it never can. That makes armed alerts a product statement in their own right, and it sets a standard any automatic route must meet: the permission is narrow enough that a hacked Sift server could not hurt users. No vendor yet demonstrably meets that standard for "sell, but never below this price".

The second shift is about expectations. On pump.fun coins a stop-loss is closer to a hope than a guarantee, and the platforms that market stops most heavily also recommend 30–35% slippage. A Sift that shows the worst-case fill, defaults to stop-limit, and says "may not fill on a rug" will look less powerful than GMGN on day one, and will be more honest about what any stop can do.
